The Problem

Where you land decides if you survive

Near the lunar south pole, the Sun and Earth skim the horizon. A ridge only a few degrees high can decide whether a lander has power, direct communication with Earth, both, or neither. Choosing the wrong spot can end a mission in hours instead of days.

Our Approach

Three real candidate sites, compared with real NASA data

  • Peak Near Shackleton — Artemis III candidate region
  • De Gerlache ridge — peer-reviewed candidate site
  • Malapert massif — NASA notional site

Solar illumination and Earth visibility from JPL Horizons ephemerides. Terrain horizon from NASA LOLA elevation data (80 m/px).

Found It Ourselves — Then Fixed It

A real error, caught and corrected with rigor

Our first Shackleton coordinate pointed to the crater floor — a permanently shadowed region where the Sun never reaches — not the candidate area near the rim. We replaced it using peer-reviewed literature. We also found our 30 km horizon radius underestimated terrain blocking; extending it to 150 km changed the clearest direction by up to +4.38° at the most affected site.

Validation

Our Differentiator

A local backup link, and one number to compare sites

When direct line-of-sight to Earth is lost, a local backup link — radiofrequency or optical — keeps a relay path alive. We combine sunlight, direct communication, and backup availability into a single team-proposed Suitability Index (S) per site, so a mission planner can compare candidates at a glance.
